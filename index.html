The UFC has never been more commercially successful, with record streaming deals, blockbuster events and fandom that stretches across the world. Four data-driven investigations ask what, if anything, that success has changed for the fighters, fans and sport built underneath it.

By Will McNamee • August 2026 • 4 investigations

The context

The UFC has never been bigger.

By almost every commercial measure, the UFC is in its strongest position ever. Nothing embodies this more than the $7.7 billion deal with Paramount+ for ownership of their US streaming rights. This deal guarantees the UFC $1.1 billion a year, almost four times as much as their previous deal with ESPN. Couple that with landmark events like the UFC White House card or UFC 306 at the Sphere in Las vegas and there's no debate that the UFC has never been so dominant in its field.

None of that is in dispute. The more interesting question is what this success has changed about the organisation, its priorities and the way it operates.

Commercial efficiency vs. Sporting & fan value

So, is the UFC suffering from success?

Not in the conventional sense. Taken purely as a business, the UFC is thriving and the fact they're able to make some of the decisions that have contributed to these issues evidences that. A struggling promotion would do everything in their power to ensure their fans were satisfied. This means booking the biggest fighters possible as soon as they're available, hosting an equitable distribution of events to ensure all fans have the opportunity to attend events, and managing sporting issues so they don't impact the integirty of the organisation.

The evidence across all four investigations suggests the UFC has transcended beyond the day-to-day priorities of a normal fight promotion. Instead, the UFC sees itself more as a business rather than a sporting organisation and their new entrepreneurial approach doesn't always align with the interests of fighters, fans and the sport itself.

Increasingly prioritised

  • Commercial efficiency
  • Media & broadcast commitments
  • Revenue & scalability
  • Entertainment value

Sometimes at odds with

  • Fighter autonomy
  • Live-event atmosphere
  • Accessibility for fans
  • Sporting continuity & meritocracy

This evidence is not one-sided. The Apex is not an inherently bad venue and the fans interviewed were more forgiving of it than online reaction would suggest. Ticket-prices are increasing, but if events are still selling out shows then what's stopping the UFC from doing this. Divisional congestion and the lack of the biggest stars isn't stopping the UFC either. Viewership figures suggest the UFC itself is the main draw now and fans will tune in just to watch a UFC show.

The strength of this project isn't any single finding. It's the pattern across four independent tests, each built to challenge a popular assumption rather than confirm it.

The UFC isn't failing because it became successful. The question is whether its definition of success has quietly changed.
